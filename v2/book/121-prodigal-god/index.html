From the shelf

First off, as an English major I am embarrassed to admit that I did not know the correct definition of the word prodigal. I always thought it referred to someone who screws up and comes back around, hat in hand. I couldn't be further from the truth — prodigal means someone who is wastefully extravagant, who spends their resources recklessly and freely. So in this story what you really have is a parable about two lost sons and a reckless (with his love) father. Keller's reorientation of the story from its traditional focus on the wayward son gives one more accurate insight into just who this tale was for. In context, Jesus' main audience was the 'older brothers' — the self-righteous pharisees who sought to control their own salvation through rigorous adherence to the law. One of best parts of Prodigal God is that it turns the reader outward showing that recognition of God's costly grace should spur his followers to serve the poor, to love the orphans and widows in society today. On my yearly re-reading list.

“The targets of the [prodigal son story] are not wayward sinners but religious people who do everything the Bible requires.”
